# Governing AI's Labour Market Transition: A Policy Proposal

**To:** Deputy Secretary, Employment & Skills  
**From:** Policy Analysis Division  
**Date:** May 2026  
**Classification:** For Official Use  

---

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is restructuring the labour market faster than existing institutions can absorb. The disruption is not uniform: it concentrates on routine cognitive tasks, middle-wage occupations, and geographically exposed communities while generating gains for high-skill workers and capital owners. Without active policy, this trajectory will deepen wage polarisation, erode public trust, and leave displaced workers without viable pathways. This brief defines the problem, assesses workforce impacts, evaluates three policy options, and recommends a coordinated package with a phased implementation plan.

---

## Problem Definition & Context

AI is changing work through three distinct channels.

**Task automation** replaces *routine* cognitive tasks — those sufficiently codifiable to be replicated algorithmically, including document review, data entry, and call-centre triage — within occupations that are not eliminated wholesale. This task-level disruption is economically significant because it shifts the skill mix required within roles without triggering the occupational displacement that unemployment statistics capture (Autor et al., 2003; Autor, 2019).

**Augmentation and complementarity** raises the productivity of workers who can direct, interpret, or quality-check AI outputs. This creates a within-occupation divergence: workers who adapt earn more; those who cannot are compressed toward lower-value residual tasks.

**Data and platform concentration** structurally advantages firms with large proprietary datasets, compounding market power over time and locking out smaller employers from the productivity gains AI generates (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018). The result is an economy bifurcated between AI-integrated, high-productivity sectors and stagnating sectors with limited access to the technology.

Standard economic theory identifies two counterforces to displacement: a *productivity effect* (cheaper AI-enabled production raises output demand, supporting labour in non-automated tasks) and a *reinstatement effect* (new labour-intensive tasks emerge). Both appear weaker in the current wave — partly because AI may constitute what Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019) term *so-so automation*, substituting labour without generating sufficient productivity gains to offset displacement. This asymmetry is the central policy problem.

Current policy architecture — designed around occupational displacement and firm-level bargaining — is mismatched to these channels. The adjustment mechanisms exist; they are simply aimed at the wrong target.

## Analysis of Impacts

### Employment and Tasks

Aggregate employment effects are modest in the short run but structurally significant over the medium term. The OECD (2023) estimates 27 percent of jobs are at high risk of significant task change; full elimination risk is concentrated in a narrower 14 percent. The more important story is compositional: administrative, logistics, and routine financial roles are contracting; care, infrastructure, AI-oversight, and technical trades roles are expanding. Workers cannot easily move between these categories without institutional support.

### Wages

Wage polarisation is the clearest distributional consequence. The hollowing out of middle-skill routine roles while demand grows at both high and low skill levels is well-documented empirically across advanced economies (Goos et al., 2014). McKinsey Global Institute (2023) projects, under accelerated adoption scenarios, that generative AI could automate tasks equivalent to 60–70 percent of employee time in knowledge-work sectors, accelerating this dynamic. Workers without the capital or institutional support to reskill are structurally locked into the compressing segment. This widens the gap between aggregate productivity growth and median wage growth: in competitive labour markets gains would flow to workers, but rising employer concentration and declining union coverage create a wedge that allows gains to accrue to capital and high-skill complementors instead.

### Industrial Structure and Equity

AI-driven productivity gains are accruing disproportionately to firms with compute and data advantages — primarily large platforms and incumbents. Dauth et al. (2021) find that industrial robot exposure in German manufacturing caused persistent regional wage depression — indicative for AI, though robots and cognitive automation have distinct task profiles. The distributional and geographic equity implications are severe: the workers and communities most exposed to displacement are least positioned to capture the gains of AI adoption.

## Policy Options

Three policy options are evaluated against four criteria: **effectiveness** (labour market impact), **equity** (distributional benefit), **fiscal cost**, and **implementation feasibility**. Each targets a distinct market failure: Option 1 addresses human capital underinvestment arising from workers' inability to pledge future wages as collateral; Option 2 addresses bargaining asymmetries at the point of restructuring; Option 3 addresses natural monopoly dynamics in data accumulation.

| Option | Effectiveness | Equity | Fiscal Cost | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. National Reskilling Fund** | High — addresses supply-side skill gap directly | High — targets displaced and low-wage workers | Medium — levy-funded, scalable | Medium — requires delivery infrastructure |
| **2. Labour Market Adjustment Mechanisms** | Medium-high — effective for cyclical shocks; effectiveness for structural technological displacement is more contested | Lower-medium — benefits incumbents in stable employment; limited reach to already-displaced workers | Low — short-time work subsidies time-limited | High — adapts existing schemes |
| **3. Competition & Data Policy Reform** | Medium — structural, long-run effect | High — prevents concentration from compounding inequality | Low — regulatory cost only | Low — complex, multi-agency, slow |

**Option 1 — National Reskilling Fund:** A levy on AI-derived productivity gains finances portable, modular credentials in high-demand fields. Credentials are tied to individuals, not employers, preserving benefit continuity during transitions. Regional delivery through employment centres addresses geographic exposure.

**Option 2 — Labour Market Adjustment Mechanisms:** Mandatory 90-day advance notification before AI-driven reductions. Short-time work subsidies incentivise reduced hours; wage insurance bridges income gaps during reskilling.

**Option 3 — Competition & Data Policy Reform:** Mandatory data-sharing obligations for dominant platforms and updated merger review criteria to assess AI-specific market power including training data control. Addresses structural inequality without direct fiscal outlay but operates on a long institutional timeline.

## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan

No single option is sufficient. The recommended package combines all three in a deliberate sequence: adjustment mechanisms are legislated first because regulatory change is faster than building new delivery infrastructure; the National Reskilling Fund scales once pilot centres establish that infrastructure; and structural data reform follows, operating on the longer institutional timeline it requires.

| Phase | Timeline | Actions | Responsible Agency | Estimated Cost | KPIs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Foundation** | Year 1 | Legislate 90-day notification; launch pilot reskilling centres in 5 high-exposure regions | DESE, Treasury | $200M (pilot) | Enrolments, transition time |
| **2 — Scale** | Years 2–3 | National Reskilling Fund operational; short-time work scheme retooled | DESE, ATO | $1.2B/yr (levy-funded) | Credential completions, wage recovery rate |
| **3 — Structural** | Years 3–5 | Data-sharing obligations enacted; merger review criteria updated; sectoral bargaining frameworks include AI governance | ACCC, Fair Work | Regulatory only | HHI trends, SME AI adoption rates |

**Key Performance Indicators** for the full package: (1) share of displaced workers re-employed at equivalent wage within 24 months, target 60% by Year 3 against a projected 40% no-policy baseline; (2) regional wage dispersion index held at or below 2024 baseline by Year 5; (3) SME AI productivity adoption rate doubling by Year 5; (4) credential completion rates by income quartile, targeting parity across bottom two quartiles by Year 4.

## Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Levy avoidance by large platforms structuring gains offshore | Medium | Design levy on domestic revenue, coordinate with OECD Pillar Two framework |
| Reskilling programmes produce credentials without labour demand | Medium | Tie funding to employer co-design; publish real-time labour demand signals |
| Data-sharing obligations face legal challenge | High | Stage implementation; model on EU Data Act precedent with clear safe harbours |
| Political resistance from industries facing adjustment obligations | High | Phased compliance timelines; recognition programmes for early adopters |
| Gains concentrate in high-skill workers even within new sectors | Medium | Embed equity conditions in fund eligibility; monitor by income quartile |
